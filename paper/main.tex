\begin{abstract}
Networks that combine several MRI sequences into an image of another sequence produce outputs whose errors are invisible to the reader, and in multi-site settings the data needed to calibrate those errors stays at the sites. We study distribution-free uncertainty for a federated model that predicts FLAIR from T1 and T2 on BraTS~2020 (365 patients, three tumour-grade-skewed clients). Because FLAIR is never an input, the residual $|f-\text{FLAIR}|$ is a genuine predictive error rather than a quantity computable at test time. We provide (i) an \emph{exact} patient-level guarantee --- with probability $\geq 1-\alpha$ a new patient has $\geq 90\,\%$ of its brain pixels inside the interval --- that does not rely on pixel independence, (ii) federated calibration in which clients release only integer counts, yielding thresholds bit-identical to pooled calibration (135/135 checks, $\leq 62$ rounds), and (iii) conformal risk control of the miss rate on the tumour boundary, which needs tumour labels only at calibration. On 54 held-out test patients and 5 FedAvg seeds, patient-level coverage at $\alpha{=}0.10$ is $0.941$ (Clopper--Pearson 95\,\% CI $[0.84, 0.99]$); pooling lets the smallest client (14 patients) use intervals 29\,\% narrower than it can calibrate alone and enables $\alpha{=}0.05$, which it cannot reach alone; risk control lowers tumour-boundary miscoverage from $0.109$ to $0.079$. Input degradation is absorbed by matched recalibration, whereas clean thresholds under-cover degraded inputs ($0.867$ at $\alpha{=}0.10$). A negative finding: splitting pixels into input-defined interior/boundary regions does not narrow intervals, because errors concentrate in lesions, not at input edges.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Clinical brain-tumour MRI protocols acquire several sequences (T1, T1ce, T2, FLAIR), and networks that combine available sequences into another --- variously framed as multi-sequence fusion or missing-sequence synthesis~\cite{dar2019pgan,zhang2021ifcnn,tang2022matr} --- are attractive when a sequence is missing, corrupted or too costly to acquire. Their failure mode is dangerous precisely because the output looks plausible: an under-estimated oedema or a smoothed lesion margin cannot be detected by inspecting the image. A deployable model should therefore attach to each output an error bar with a statistical guarantee, and in a multi-site setting it must be trained and calibrated without moving patient images between hospitals~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}.

Split conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005alrw,lei2018distribution} gives distribution-free, finite-sample guarantees and has been extended to image-to-image regression~\cite{angelopoulos2022im2im} and to risk control over images~\cite{angelopoulos2022crc,teneggi2023krcps}. Three issues arise when it is used for multi-sequence MRI. (a) The guarantee is only meaningful if the target is not a function of the inputs: if the ``ground truth'' is, e.g., a weighted average of the input sequences, the residual can be computed exactly at test time and a conformal interval for it is vacuous. (b) Pixels within an image are strongly dependent, so a pixel-level quantile does not carry the usual exchangeability guarantee. (c) Calibration data are held by different sites with different case mixes; small sites may not have enough patients to calibrate alone~\cite{lu2023fedcp}.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{held-out-target protocol} for multi-sequence MRI on BraTS~2020: T1 and T2 are the inputs and FLAIR, never seen at inference, is the target, so the conformal score is a genuine predictive residual.
    \item \textbf{Two clearly separated guarantee levels}: an exact patient-level guarantee whose exchangeable unit is the patient, and approximate pixel-level per-region (Mondrian~\cite{vovk2005alrw}) thresholds that we validate empirically and label as such.
    \item \textbf{Count-only federated calibration} that reproduces the pooled conformal quantile bit-for-bit, and evidence that its main value is for small clients: per-client calibration is wider or infeasible at small $n$.
    \item \textbf{Conformal risk control on the tumour boundary}, using tumour labels only at calibration, which brings the boundary miss rate below the target where plain thresholds exceed it.
    \item \textbf{Honest negative findings}: input-defined boundary regions do not localise the model's errors, and a random-projection update compressor we previously proposed does not train at all (Section~\ref{ssec:frop}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Multi-sequence fusion and synthesis.} IFCNN~\cite{zhang2021ifcnn} and U2Fusion~\cite{xu2022u2fusion} are general CNN fusion frameworks; MATR~\cite{tang2022matr} uses a multiscale adaptive Transformer for multimodal medical image fusion; conditional GANs synthesise missing MRI contrasts from available ones~\cite{dar2019pgan}. Federated multimodal medical image fusion has recently been studied by FLMMIF~\cite{meng2026flmmif} and FEMMIF~\cite{alabduljabbar2026femmif}; neither provides calibrated uncertainty. We deliberately use a small, standard backbone (IFCNN) because our contribution is the uncertainty layer, not the predictor.

\noindent\textbf{Conformal prediction for images.} Split conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005alrw,lei2018distribution} calibrates a threshold on held-out scores. Angelopoulos \textit{et al.}~\cite{angelopoulos2022im2im} give image-to-image intervals that, with high probability, bound the expected fraction of pixels falling outside them; conformal risk control (CRC)~\cite{angelopoulos2022crc} controls the expectation of any bounded monotone loss, and K-RCPS~\cite{teneggi2023krcps} optimises spatially varying intervals for diffusion models. Mondrian conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005alrw} calibrates separately within categories, which is the basis of our per-region thresholds.

\noindent\textbf{Federated learning and conformal prediction.} FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg} and FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox} train shared models without moving data; secure aggregation~\cite{bonawitz2017secagg} reveals only sums of client messages. Lu \textit{et al.}~\cite{lu2023fedcp} study conformal prediction when calibration data are distributed across clients under partial exchangeability. Our federated calibration targets the exact pooled quantile with integer messages only.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task and model}
Each sample is an axial slice with inputs $x=(\text{T1},\text{T2})$ and target $y=\text{FLAIR}$, all scaled to $[0,1]$. The predictor $f$ is a two-stream IFCNN~\cite{zhang2021ifcnn} (316\,k parameters, element-wise max fusion, sigmoid output) whose output is multiplied by the input-derived brain mask $M=(\text{T1}>0)\lor(\text{T2}>0)$. Without this mask a sigmoid can never output exactly 0 on the $\approx 73\,\%$ air background, where trivial baselines are exactly 0. The loss is $\|f-y\|_1 + (1-\text{SSIM}(f,y))$.

\subsection{Federated training}
$K$ clients run FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}: each round every client trains one local epoch on a random subset of its slices starting from the global weights, and the server averages the weight deltas, including BatchNorm running statistics. A centralised reference uses the same trainer with all client training data pooled into one client.

\subsection{Scores and input-only regions}
The score of pixel $i$ is $r_i=|f(x)_i-y_i|$. Each pixel receives a region label computed from the inputs only: \emph{background} ($i\notin M$), \emph{boundary} (the 25\,\% of brain pixels with the largest gradient magnitude of the Gaussian-smoothed ($\sigma{=}1$) guide $\tfrac12(\text{T1}+\text{T2})$), and \emph{interior} (the rest of $M$). Fixing the boundary share avoids dataset-specific edge thresholds; Canny~\cite{canny1986edge} with fixed thresholds 50/100 is reported as a sensitivity check.

\subsection{Two guarantee levels}
For scores $s_1,\dots,s_n$ the split-conformal threshold is the order statistic $\hat q_\alpha=s_{(k)}$ with $k=\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$ ($\hat q_\alpha=\infty$ if $k>n$)~\cite{lei2018distribution}.

\emph{Patient level (exact).} For patient $p$ and region $R$, let $s_p$ be the smallest $t$ such that a fraction $\geq 1-\beta$ of $\{r_i : i \in R \text{ in any slice of } p\}$ is $\leq t$, and let $\hat\tau=\hat q_\alpha(s_1,\dots,s_n)$ over $n$ calibration patients.
\begin{proposition}
If the calibration patients and a new patient are exchangeable, then with probability at least $1-\alpha$ the new patient has at least a fraction $1-\beta$ of its region-$R$ pixels inside $[f-\hat\tau, f+\hat\tau]$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By construction $s_{n+1}\le\hat\tau$ holds exactly when the covered fraction at $\hat\tau$ is $\geq 1-\beta$; the standard split-conformal argument~\cite{vovk2005alrw,lei2018distribution} gives $\Pr(s_{n+1}\le\hat\tau)\ge 1-\alpha$.
\end{proof}
The unit of exchangeability is the patient, so dependence between pixels, and between slices of one patient, is irrelevant. We use $\beta=0.10$ and report the guarantee per region (a union bound gives a joint statement).

\emph{Pixel level (approximate).} Per-region thresholds $\hat\tau_R=\hat q_\alpha$ over all calibration pixels of region $R$ (Mondrian calibration~\cite{vovk2005alrw}). Pixels are dependent, so the nominal pixel coverage is \emph{not} guaranteed; we validate it empirically with a patient-cluster bootstrap. The baseline is one global threshold over all brain pixels.

\subsection{Federated calibration with counts only}
Client $k$ holds non-negative scores and exposes only its size $n_k$ and counts $c_k(t)=\#\{s\le t\}$. The server binary-searches the IEEE-754 bit pattern of $t$, which is order-preserving for non-negative floats, keeping $\sum_k c_k(\text{lo})<k\le\sum_k c_k(\text{hi})$. After at most 64 rounds of one integer per client the result equals the pooled order statistic exactly. With secure aggregation~\cite{bonawitz2017secagg} the server sees only $\sum_k c_k(t)$; what is revealed is the pooled rank of each probed threshold, and no individual score. CRC (below) federates trivially: clients send per-$\lambda$ sums of their losses.

\subsection{Tumour-boundary risk control}
Plain thresholds treat all pixels of a region alike, but the clinically important errors lie at lesion margins, which input-only regions need not capture. For calibration patient $p$ let $B_p$ be the tumour-boundary band (the expert segmentation dilated minus eroded by one pixel) and define the loss
\begin{equation}
L_p(\lambda)=\frac{1}{|B_p|}\,\Big|\{\,i\in B_p:\; r_i>\lambda\,\hat\tau_{R(i)}\}\Big|,
\end{equation}
which is non-increasing in $\lambda$ and bounded by 1, where $\hat\tau_{R(i)}$ is the pixel-level threshold of $i$'s region. CRC~\cite{angelopoulos2022crc} selects $\hat\lambda=\inf\{\lambda: \tfrac{n}{n+1}\hat R_n(\lambda)+\tfrac{1}{n+1}\le\alpha\}$ with $\hat R_n$ the mean calibration loss, which guarantees $\mathbb{E}[L_{n+1}(\hat\lambda)]\le\alpha$ for a new exchangeable patient. The deployed interval $f\pm\hat\lambda\hat\tau_{R(i)}$ uses inputs only; segmentation labels are needed only at calibration.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\noindent\textbf{Data.} BraTS~2020 training data~\cite{menze2015brats,bakas2017brats} as redistributed on Kaggle (\url{kaggle.com/datasets/awsaf49/brats20-dataset-training-validation}). Of 369 patients, 365 have at least one axial slice with $\geq 1\,\%$ tumour; we keep those 17{,}568 slices. Each sequence volume is clipped to its $[0.5, 99.5]$ brain-voxel percentiles, scaled to $[0,1]$ (background exactly 0), and resized to $128{\times}128$.

\noindent\textbf{Federated split (patient level).} Patients are partitioned by grade into $K{=}3$ non-IID clients: client~0 is HGG-only (146 patients), client~1 mixed (98 HGG + 13 LGG), client~2 LGG-only (54). Within each client, patients are split 65/10/25\,\% into train (95/72/35), validation (15/11/5; learning curves only) and calibration (36/28/14). A further 54 patients (45 HGG, 9 LGG) form a central test set. No patient appears in two splits.

\noindent\textbf{Training.} $T{=}30$ rounds, one local epoch of 1{,}000 random slices per client per round, batch 16, Adam (lr $10^{-3}$); centralised: 3{,}000 slices per round. The protocol was fixed in advance and the round-30 model is always used, with no model selection. Five seeds $\{42,1,2,3,4\}$. A round takes $\approx 32$\,s on a Colab T4; FedAvg uploads 3.8\,MB per round (three clients). Baselines: zero image, copy of T2, $\tfrac12$(T1+T2), and a least-squares fit $a\text{T1}+b\text{T2}+c$ on 60 training patients.

\noindent\textbf{Conformal evaluation.} All conformal results use the 5 FedAvg models; we report mean (and std) over seeds. $\alpha\in\{0.05,0.10,0.15\}$, $\beta=0.10$, CRC at $\alpha=0.10$ on the grid $\lambda\in\{0.01,\dots,10\}$. Calibration schemes: \emph{pooled} (all 78 calibration patients, computed federatedly) and \emph{per-client}. Patient-level coverage is reported with Clopper--Pearson intervals~\cite{clopper1934}; pixel coverage with a patient-cluster bootstrap (2{,}000 resamples). Input degradation applies Gaussian blur ($\sigma_b$) and additive noise ($\sigma_n$) to T1 and T2 inside the brain mask, with the same noise realisation for every seed.

\subsection{Exact patient-level coverage}
Table~\ref{tab:patient} confirms the patient-level guarantee at every $\alpha$: e.g.\ at $\alpha{=}0.10$, $94\,\%$ of test patients have $\geq 90\,\%$ of their brain pixels covered, and every Clopper--Pearson interval contains the target. The cost is width: $\hat\tau\approx0.34$ on a $[0,1]$ intensity scale for the brain region. Because the guarantee must hold for 90\,\% of a patient's pixels, including lesion pixels with large errors, the bound is honest but loose.

\subsection{Federated vs.\ per-client calibration}
The count-only protocol reproduced the pooled threshold bit-for-bit in all 135 checks (5 seeds $\times$ 3 input conditions $\times$ 3 regions $\times$ 3 $\alpha$), using at most 62 rounds. Table~\ref{tab:schemes} shows where pooling matters. The two larger clients obtain thresholds and coverage close to pooled. Client~2 (14 LGG patients) can calibrate alone at $\alpha{=}0.10$ only because $k=n=14$: its threshold is the largest calibration score, so its intervals are 40\,\% wider than pooled ($0.479$ vs.\ $0.342$), i.e.\ pooled intervals are 29\,\% narrower, and at $\alpha{=}0.05$ it cannot calibrate at all ($k>n$, $\hat\tau=\infty$). We did \emph{not} observe the grade-shift failure one might expect: HGG-only calibration covers the LGG test patients as well as pooled calibration does (8 of 9 in every scheme). With 9 LGG test patients the data cannot resolve differences of this size.

\subsection{Does an input-defined spatial split help?}
Table~\ref{tab:mondrian} compares per-region pixel thresholds with one global brain threshold. With gradient regions, the boundary threshold is \emph{smaller} than the interior one ($0.244$ vs.\ $0.274$), and the brain-average half-width is unchanged ($0.75\cdot0.274+0.25\cdot0.244=0.267$ vs.\ global $0.268$). Splitting merely redistributes width. The reason is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:example}: the strongest input gradients lie on the brain outline, which the masked model reproduces well, while the large errors are lesion hyperintensities in the ``interior''. Only 30--33\,\% of tumour-boundary pixels fall in the gradient boundary region (72--74\,\% for Canny, whose boundary region however covers 58--63\,\% of the brain). All pixel-level coverages are above nominal ($0.93$--$0.95$ at a target of $0.90$). This is consistent with pixel coverage carrying no guarantee and with the calibration and test patients coming from slightly different pools. We therefore do not claim that input-defined spatial splitting improves efficiency; the clinically relevant spatial structure is addressed by risk control instead.

\subsection{Tumour-boundary risk control}
Without risk control ($\lambda{=}1$), the per-region thresholds leave $10.9\,\%$ of tumour-boundary pixels uncovered on clean inputs and $14.3\,\%$ under strong degradation, above the $10\,\%$ target (Table~\ref{tab:crc}). CRC with pooled calibration selects $\hat\lambda\approx1.1$--$1.2$ and brings the realised risk to $0.076$--$0.083$, without using any labels at test time. Per-client CRC is again noisier. Client~1's realised risk is $0.103{\pm}0.008$, slightly above $\alpha$; this is allowed because the guarantee holds in expectation over calibration sets, not for every realisation.

\input{tables/r2_crc}

\subsection{Input degradation}
When calibration patients are degraded in the same way as test inputs, coverage stays close to its clean value and the half-widths grow with degradation: boundary $0.244\to0.265\to0.282$, interior $0.274\to0.290\to0.299$ (Table~\ref{tab:degradation}). Applying clean thresholds to degraded inputs breaks the exchangeability assumption, and coverage falls to $0.867$/$0.882$ under strong degradation, with bootstrap intervals below $0.90$. In practice the guarantee is only as good as the match between calibration and deployment acquisition; monitoring input quality and recalibrating per acquisition condition is required.

\subsection{Negative result: random-projection update compression}
\label{ssec:frop}
An earlier version of this work proposed compressing each client's weight delta by a fixed random orthonormal projection to $d{=}32$ coefficients (``FROP''). With a fixed basis $P\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}$, every aggregated update lies in the row space of $P$, so after any number of rounds the global model is the initialisation plus a $d$-dimensional perturbation. Here $d/N\approx 6.5\times10^{-6}$ for the 4.9\,M-parameter backbone used then. Our logs confirm that this model does not train: evaluation SSIM was $0.23936$ at round 1 and $0.23936$ at round 50, and the same compressor applied to IFCNN moved SSIM from $0.239$ to $0.234$. The implementation also projected BatchNorm running statistics, freezing them at initialisation; this is fixed in the released code. Its empirical resistance to gradient inversion was therefore that of a client transmitting almost nothing, and compression alone is known to give a false sense of security~\cite{yue2023obfuscation}. We withdraw all privacy and communication claims for it. This paper claims no privacy property beyond the data locality of federated training.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{ssec:limitations}
\textbf{(L1) Scope.} One dataset, 2D slices, tumour-bearing slices only, and one predictor; the 54-patient test set has only 9 LGG patients. \textbf{(L2) Width.} Patient-level half-widths of $\approx0.34$ are wide; a stronger predictor or a locally adaptive score (e.g.\ normalised by an ensemble spread) should tighten them without affecting validity. \textbf{(L3) Exchangeability.} All guarantees assume calibration and test patients are exchangeable; Table~\ref{tab:degradation} shows the failure when acquisition differs. \textbf{(L4) Federation is simulated} in one process; secure aggregation is assumed, not implemented, and the counts at probed thresholds reveal pooled ranks. \textbf{(L5) Task framing.} Predicting a held-out sequence is closer to cross-sequence synthesis than to classical fusion, which has no ground truth; we chose it precisely because it gives the conformal score a meaning. No reader study assesses clinical usefulness.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

For federated prediction of a held-out MRI sequence, distribution-free uncertainty is achievable and useful when its guarantees are stated at the right level. The exact patient-level guarantee holds and does not require pixel independence. Federated calibration with counts only reproduces pooled thresholds bit-for-bit and spares small sites wide or infeasible intervals. Conformal risk control brings the miss rate at tumour margins below target using labels only at calibration. Two cautions emerged: input-defined spatial regions do not localise the model's errors, and guarantees fail when deployment inputs are degraded relative to calibration. Locally adaptive scores, full 3D evaluation and federated recalibration under acquisition shift are the natural next steps.
